# How DJs and producers think about remixes

Research done 2026-10-28 at the person's request, after they heard four remixes of "the curse of curves" and said the system keeps making
the same format whatever the song: "the style, structure, drops and overall production needs to be varied and creative and inventive, not
locked to the same format." This page collects what producers, DJs, labels and music theorists say about how a remix is planned and built,
and what that means for the co-producer. Every claim names its source; the list with links is at the end.

What this research could not do: I cannot listen to audio on SoundCloud, YouTube, Beatport or DJcity. Where it compares a remix with its
original, it uses published data (tempo, key, length, genre from Mixgraph, Wikipedia and label pages) and what producers and writers say.
Listening comparisons start when the person brings originals and remixes as files (the study protocol at the end).

## 1. What a remix is for

- **To take a record to an audience it would not otherwise reach.** Simon Langford, who remixed Rihanna, Robbie Williams and the Sugababes,
  puts this first, and lists who a remix must satisfy in order: the club crowd, the DJs, the promoters, the artist and the label, and the
  remixer last (Sound On Sound, The Remix Business, part 1).
- **To change the context, not just the beat.** Nick Muir: aim the track at a different setting, a peak-time record reworked for an
  afternoon or a boat party, or the other way round; a different genre or tempo reaches DJs who did not like the original (Electronic Groove).
- **To improve the original for the dancefloor**, Michael Mayer's view, and **to take an already great idea further**, Honey Dijon's;
  a bad remix "adds nothing and loses the original's magic" (Attack Magazine, The Art of the Remix).
- **To reframe a song for listeners who have not heard that angle**: Oppidan calls remixing a "one-way collaboration" (MusicTech, How we
  remixed Fred again..).
- Remixes often outgrow their originals, which is the strongest sign that a remix is a new reading and not a beat swap: Felix Jaehn's
  "Cheerleader" (a reggae-fusion song made into tropical deep house, number one in 20 countries including the US Hot 100), Robin Schulz's
  "Waves" (it debuted at number one in the UK), Todd Terry's "Missing" (an album track that became the "ultimate club-pop
  crossover"), MK's "Push the Feeling On" (the original reached UK 86; the dub reached UK 3 and the band deleted the original).

## 2. The kinds of remix and version

| Kind | What it starts from and changes | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Official remix | Commissioned; stems or MIDI supplied; the remixer reworks the artist's own material | MusicRadar, 6 types of remix |
| Bootleg | Unofficial, usually from the stereo mix | MusicRadar |
| Mash-up | One track's vocal over another's instrumental | MusicRadar |
| VIP | The producer's own new version of their track (new riffs, drums or vocal) | MusicRadar; Toolroom |
| Radio or club edit | The same song cut for a market: Get Lucky's four-minute edit | MusicRadar |
| Re-edit | The finished stereo mix rearranged: longer drops, simpler form, the bridge removed | MusicRadar; DJ TechTools |
| Dub mix | Like the club mix with less vocal | Sound On Sound part 1 |
| PA or TV edit | A radio edit with the lead vocal removed or low, for live performance | Sound On Sound part 1 |
| DJ pool versions | Intro (8 bars added to start and end), hook first, short edit, transition (tempo change into the track), clean, instrumental, acapella | DJcity support |
| Redrum | A club kick under a slow pop or R&B song at a club tempo; criticised for quantising the soul out of the performance | Midnight Rebels |

A useful line between remix and re-edit: a remix is "a new interpretation created from the individual parts of a song"; a re-edit
rearranges the finished recording, which "vastly reduces the creative licence" (Harold Heath, DJ TechTools).

Eduardo Navas's three forms give the scale of change: **extended** (longer, more mixable: Walter Gibbons stretching "Ten Percent" to ten
minutes), **selective** (material added and removed while the core stays: Coldcut's "Paid in Full"), and **reflexive** (the remix claims
its own autonomy while the original stays recognisable: Mad Professor's "No Protection").

A label job is several versions at once: an extended club mix, a dub mix, an instrumental and a radio edit, sometimes a PA edit (Langford).

## 3. How much of the original to keep

There is no single answer; the best-known remixers sit at different points, and the same remixer moves with the song.

- **Keep almost everything, change the setting.** Ewan Pearson keeps as much as he can and makes it work in a club "without losing its
  identity"; Michael Mayer uses as many original elements as possible "as respect" (Attack). Todd Terry kept Tracey Thorn's whole vocal and
  Ben Watt's melodies and put them on "swung NYC house" with "hard-ass beats" (DJ Mag).
- **Keep one thing.** MK sometimes keeps a phrase, sometimes nothing (Attack). On "Push the Feeling On" he used one vocal line out of the
  chorus, the one most listeners did not pick out, chopped into "almost continuously looped gibberish" (Wikipedia, quoting Music Week and
  the singer). Chris Lake kept a short guitar run from Taylor Swift's "Opalite" and made it the drop (MusicTech, 2026). Honey Dijon
  sometimes keeps only a synth part or the bassline, or makes a dub (Attack).
- **The limits.** Too many recognisable elements and it is a bootleg, not a remix (Toolroom Academy); too few and clients reject it: Honey
  Dijon had mixes turned down for using too little of the original or not enough vocal (Attack). Purple Disco Machine will not "do
  something that's completely different with only a couple of elements of the original" (Official Charts). HAAi's first version of a Fred
  again.. remix came back with "you respected me too much" (MusicTech).
- **The working rule of thumb** from producer guides: delete about half the stems; keep the vocal, atmospheres and the distinctive sounds;
  replace the drums, but one distinctive original drum sound can stay (Hyperbits). Keep the "same DNA" (chords, lead vocal, hook or overall
  vibe) and rework the rest (Loopcloud).

## 4. The anchor: what carries the song's identity

A remix is built around one or two anchors, and choosing them is the first creative decision.

- **The vocal** is the usual anchor (MK, Todd Edwards: "a soulful female vocal is a strong starting point"); Hyperbits advises soloing the
  vocal first because it "reveals your remix direction".
- **An overlooked element can become the hook**: "pick the central hook, either the original's or a new element nobody noticed before"
  (Loopcloud); MK's unnoticed chorus line; Lake's guitar run.
- **A weak vocal can push the music darker and more inventive** (Todd Edwards, Attack).
- **Respect the meaning**: do not cut words out of context in ways that disrespect the song (Langford, part 3); changes should fit the
  lyrics and mood (Hyperbits).

## 5. Tempo, key and harmony

- **Tempo bands.** A song in the mid-90s bpm is awkward for a 128 house remix: speed it up to 128 or halve it to 64. Ballads usually need
  speeding up; bouncy, rhythmic songs can often be slowed; a vocal with strong vibrato sounds chipmunked about 30% faster, and slowing one
  that much makes it sleepy (Langford, part 2). Vocals take about a 10% stretch comfortably; past that, phrasing can feel odd (Langford,
  part 3). Speeding up usually sounds better than slowing down, and under about 20 bpm of change is generally safe (Berklee Online).
- **Published examples** (tempo, key and genre as Mixgraph lists them; detected by its analysis, not checked by ear):

  | Version | bpm | Key | Genre |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Disclosure, You & Me (original, 2013) | 130 | Bb minor | pop / UK garage |
  | Flume Remix (Mixgraph lists 2016) | 150 | Bb minor | trap / future bass (a half-time feel) |
  | Rivo Remix (2024) | 122 | Bb minor | afro house (the vocal slowed about 6%, if the listed tempos are right) |
  | CamelPhat and Elderbrook, Cola (original, 2017) | 122 | Bb major | house |
  | Made By Pete Extended Remix (2024) | 122 | Eb major | melodic house and techno |
  | ARTBAT Extended Remix (2025) | 125 | F minor | tech house, for "big festivals" |

  Cola alone has at least eleven official remixes, from Elderbrook's own 2:52 "Chilled mix" to Franky Rizardo's 8:24 club mix, through
  Mousse T's disco "Glitterbox mix", Robin Schulz, MoBlack and Zhu (Wikipedia). One song, many genuinely different readings.
- **Key.** Keep the original key where possible; swapping to the relative major or minor is the easiest change and "can dramatically
  alter the mood" (Langford, part 2; Hyperbits; Soundfly's "flip the mood": a bright major song made minor and darker).
- **Reharmonise.** Write new chords under the vocal (Hyperbits). Ewan Pearson "may re-harmonise or change mode" (Attack). Chris Lake was
  proudest of "a progression and a series of sounds that could work from the verse through into the chorus" of a song whose verse and
  chorus had different harmonic worlds (MusicTech). If a repeating sequence clashes with only a note or two of the vocal, change the
  chord at that point only (Langford, part 3). Melodies that do not firmly establish a key are the easiest to reharmonise; Ethan Hein fits
  Bowie's "Starman" vocal to G minor, D minor, C Mixolydian and A Phrygian grooves.

## 6. Structure: a remix is not the song's form with a beat under it

- **Club form is built for mixing points and energy, not verse and chorus.** Berklee's remix template: Mix In (16 bars, little or no
  harmony), Main Beat, Melody, Build (8 to 16 bars), Breakdown, Melody with added harmony, Build, Main Beat, Mix Out (16 bars); sections in
  8-bar blocks.
- **How a 3-minute song becomes a 7 to 8 minute club mix** (Langford, part 2): 8 or 16 bars of instrumental after a chorus before the next
  verse; a breakdown of about a minute, up to 1.5 minutes if it holds interest; the last chorus repeated, or an instrumental pass before a
  final vocal; the whole on a rising "ramp" of builds and drops toward the last chorus. He starts with the chorus, the most complex section,
  then works backwards, simplifying the verses; intro and outro are the most stripped back. "Vocal-led tracks usually keep their structure"
  (MusicTech) but instrument-led or heavily reworked remixes freely lift, move, duplicate or delete sections.
- **Formal functions move.** Brad Osborn (Intégral, 2023) shows that EDM-era pop splits the old chorus into a **riserchorus** (the vocal
  hook over the build, no kick) and a **drop** (the loudest, thickest part, instrumental or with processed vocal samples). The most common
  design is verse, prechorus, riserchorus, drop; songs are three "rotations" of the same core (compound AAA) where the first is often cut
  short, the second is complete, and the third replaces the bridge with a hybrid (a riserchorus-bridge, or the hook placed over the drop
  beat). Some songs start on the riserchorus with no verse at all (Calvin Harris and Rihanna's "This Is What You Came For"). The same
  material can change function by layering alone: a sample brought forward with its highs restored turns a verse into a prechorus.
- **Restructuring moves** from producer guides: make the bridge the chorus, the second verse the intro, build the whole remix around the
  bridge, reverse a melody (Soundfly); use only the chorus, switch to half time, reach the drop quickly (Hyperbits); hook the listener in
  the first 10 to 15 seconds (Cymatics, for remix contests); trim breakdowns harder than feels natural at home, and do not make the room
  wait for the hook (Magnetic Magazine).
- **Mixability still matters**: 8 to 16 bars of clean intro and outro, section lengths in multiples of 4 or 8 bars (Berklee; Magnetic;
  DJcity's "Intro" versions add 8 bars to each end).

## 7. Drops

- A drop is "a point in a music track where a sudden change of rhythm or bass line occurs", after a build-up and a break; kinds include
  the bass drop, the pop-drop (a post-chorus that became pop's climax), the beat-up (the kick returns), a single late climax, the anti-drop
  (the chorus more minimal than the build) and consecutive drops (Wikipedia, Drop).
- In vocal genres the climax usually falls on the last chorus; in instrumental genres in the final quarter (Wikipedia).
- **Drop two must not be drop one again.** Tracks lose energy after the first drop when the drop is copied, when layers are piled on
  instead of contrast, and when there is no room to breathe; change the drum fills, voicings, percussion, bass pattern, textures or vocals,
  pull energy back before the next peak, use the best ideas now (Lost Stories Academy).
- **Moments that make a drop land**: mute everything for a bar or two and let reverb or delay tails carry it before the chorus returns; add
  one new musical element in the final chorus (Langford, part 2); tease the drop melody before the drop and build original fragments into
  it (Hyperbits).

## 8. Groove and sound

- **Drums and groove first** for many: Honey Dijon starts with drums, then the groove, sound and arrangement for tension and release, EQ
  last (Attack); Purple Disco Machine: "The most important thing for me is the groove, the beat", then the bassline (Official Charts;
  15 Questions). Langford's extended mixes start from drums and a simplified groove, often a bassline on one note.
- **Sounds in context, not solo**; bracket with high and low pass filters (Langford, part 2). Overdrive "liberally": Chris Lake puts
  overdrive after reverb and delay so the effects distort (MusicTech). HAAi used a gate (Duck) to turn a string section into a trance
  pattern; Hamdi built a harder bassline from a sound made for drum and bass (MusicTech).
- **Swap roles**: a string motif becomes the bassline (MusicRadar, 18 tips); pitch a chop so a vocal works as an instrument (Melodics,
  sample flip); chop, rearrange, reverse and re-sequence the source rather than loop it (Melodics).
- A wobble bass is a filter (and often more) moved by an LFO synced to the beat, commonly a triplet 8th, bright distortion and heavy
  compression on top (MusicRadar, LFO wobble bass). Layering a clean sub under it is common practice; that article does not cover it.

## 9. Where remixes go wrong

- The same arrangement copied and pasted section to section; layers piled on in place of contrast (Lost Stories Academy).
- Using too much of the original (a bootleg) or too little (a rejected remix) (Toolroom; Attack).
- Copying the current number one's sound, weak drums, a badly mixed bass (Toolroom Academy).
- Losing the original's soul to a grid (Midnight Rebels on redrums); vocals buried or chipmunked by careless stretching (Langford).
- Doing it without feeling: almost every producer in Attack's feature says they only take remixes they connect with; Todd Edwards says
  "the first idea is usually the best".

## 10. The DJ's side

DJs play a remix when the original will not move a floor (a slow popular song), keep the original when the crowd wants to sing along
(PCDJ). They need clean entries and exits, the hook within reach, and versions for the room (DJcity's intro, hook first, short edit and
transition versions). A remix in a new genre gets the record into sets where the original never fit (Muir).

## 11. What this means for the co-producer

**Why every remix had the same shape.** `songform.arrange` maps the song one to one: every verse becomes a groove, every chorus becomes a
drop after a build, every instrumental part a break, with the pack's intro and outro around them. The variety options only change build
policy, lengths, patterns, kits and sounds on top of that frame. So four remixes in two genres all read intro, groove, drop, groove, drop,
break, build, drop, outro. That is a selective remix of the song's own form, every time; the research says the form itself is the main
creative choice.

**Measured on the remix the person liked best** (curse house B v2, with `tools/study_remix_pair.py`, 2026-10-28): from 0:13 to 3:43 every
8-bar block of the master sits within 1 LU of the loudest; the one big contrast is at 3:43, where the track falls 9 dB into its only
breakdown and builds back into the last drop at 4:23. That is the part the person singled out ("that drop around 3:44 is great"). 20 of
the song's 35 sung lines are heard, nine as chops. The lesson matches the research: contrast makes a drop, and the arranger gave this song
one. (Corrected by the first study, section 13: a professional remix is as flat between its groove and its drops; what it has and house B
v2 lacks is a subtraction every 16 bars.)

**The person's own rule meets the research here.** On 2026-10-25 the person asked for whole verses and choruses kept with the original's
instrumentation. The remixes the research admires keep one line (MK), one guitar run (Lake), the hook over a new form (Osborn's
riserchorus and drop), or the whole vocal on a new setting (Todd Terry). The two can live together if "keep whole lines, never cut a
word" stays the rule while which lines, in what order and in what function becomes the remix's choice. The person agreed (2026-10-28).

**What to build** (proposed, in order):
1. A concept step before any pattern: the remix's purpose and room, its anchor(s), how much it keeps (extended, selective or
   reflexive), its tempo and key strategy, its harmonic strategy and its form, written into the brief and chosen per song.
2. A form designer that reassigns functions instead of copying the song's order: hook first; one verse only; the chorus as riserchorus
   over the build with a chopped or instrumental drop; an overlooked line as the drop's hook; the bridge as the breakdown; three
   rotations with the first cut short and the third altered; a single late climax; an anti-drop. Several such forms per genre, and two
   remixes of one song never share a form.
3. Reharmonisation as a real option: the relative major or minor, a new progression under the vocal checked against its notes, a chord
   changed only where it clashes.
4. Tempo strategies: the same tempo, double or half time, and a small slowdown for bouncy vocals (Rivo's You & Me is about 6% slower);
   the person (2026-10-28) allows a slowdown "but not too slow and preserve pitch": at most 6 %, pitch and formants kept.
5. Drop design per drop: drop two differs from drop one (bass pattern, voicing, drums, a new element), and the climax is planned.
6. Bass that can be the star: rolling and wobbling basses (LFO-synced filters, triplet rates changing per beat, distortion over a clean
   sub) for the house B direction the person liked.
7. A clarity check in the panel: the vocal must stay intelligible over reverb and delay (the person: house A's echo and reverb made the
   song inaudible).

## 12. Studying the person's examples

When the person brings an original and its remixes as files, each pair is studied the same way, and what is learned goes into the
`remix-craft` skill as moves with their source:
1. Tempo, key and length of both (the remix's tempo change and key change, if any).
2. Section map of both, and where each original vocal line and hook appears in the remix (by the transcribed words and their times), in
   what function (intro, build, drop, breakdown) and how treated (whole, chopped, pitched, filtered, stuttered).
3. What is new: drums, bass, chords (does the harmony change under the same melody), lead sounds, the anchor.
4. The energy curve section by section (loudness, bands, width) and how each drop differs from the last.
5. The form in Osborn's terms and in club terms (mix in, rotations, breakdown length, climax).
6. A one-paragraph reading: what the remixer decided, and the move that makes it work.

## 13. The first study: Sorry and Freejak's remix (2026-10-28)

The person brought "04 Sorry" and "Justin Bieber - Sorry - Freejak Remix (Clean)". Both were analysed whole and every bar measured; the
page is docs/remix_studies/sorry_freejak.md. What it adds to the sections above:

- **Tempo** (section 5): a professional took a 100 bpm vocal to house's 128 (x1.28), well past the 10 % rule of thumb, with pitch, key and
  formants untouched (same median note, formants within 3 %, line timing stretched uniformly). The genre's standard tempo beat the
  smallest stretch. The form option's tempo "standard" now does this.
- **Structure** (section 6): the remix kept every line in order but turned each pass's job: verse 1 on the groove, chorus 1 sung over a
  break with the bass out, the record's own vocal-chop hook as the drop, verse 2 in a break, chorus 2 on the drop. The original is itself a
  riserchorus-and-drop song (Osborn); Freejak kept the logic and changed how it lifts (into a break instead of up a build).
  songform.arrange now takes verse_as, chorus_as, drop_after and tail_break from a form option.
- **Drops** (section 7): no risers, no impacts; a stop bar with a noise sweep and the last sung words before each drop; the drop's two
  halves differ (bright bass and the hook, then a darker bass with a new off-beat layer).
- **Energy**: the drops sit within 0.5 LU of the verse groove. Contrast is made by subtraction, three breaks (1.7 to 3.6 LU down) and two
  stop bars (3 to 12 LU down) in 2:39. This corrects the reading of house B v2 in section 11: the flat level was not the fault, the one
  breakdown in 3:43 was.
- **Harmony** (section 5): a re-harmonization with a faster harmonic rhythm (IV vi V became iii IV | ii V, two chords a bar, each change a
  16th early). Harmony loops can now change chord inside the bar and replace the record's chords under the whole song when locked.
- **Groove and bass** (section 8): the record's dembow 3-3-2 survives as a dotted-8th bass and pluck (4-3-3-3-3) over a straight kick;
  the bass moved an octave up from the 808 with harmonics that carry, ducked 12 to 15 dB by each kick, the low end mono.

Built from it: seven options sourced to the study (form, drums, bass, harmony, fx, vocal, mix), the engine changes named above, the loop
and kit extraction (`extract_song_loops`, `find_matching_loops`, skill `loops-and-kits`) and the FL steps for every move
(skills/remix-craft/references/fl-howto.md).
